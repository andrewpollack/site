Caring for Residents with Cancer

Caring for Residents with Cancer

Oct 26, 202610 min read

Overview

Residents living with cancer may experience physical symptoms, treatment-related complications, emotional distress, and changes in their ability to perform daily activities.

Person-centered cancer care focuses on supporting comfort, safety, dignity, independence, and the resident’s individual goals.

Cancer experiences vary widely. Some residents continue their usual activities during treatment, while others need significant assistance.

Core Principle: Care for the person, not just the diagnosis. Follow the individualized care plan and report changes promptly.

Supporting Residents During Cancer Treatment

Fatigue

Cancer-related fatigue is persistent physical, emotional, or cognitive tiredness that may not improve fully with rest.

Possible contributors include cancer itself, anemia, treatment, disrupted sleep, poor intake, and emotional distress.

CNA Considerations:

  • Allow additional time for ADLs.
  • Schedule care around rest periods when possible.
  • Encourage permitted activity while respecting the resident’s limitations.
  • Help prioritize essential activities.
  • Observe changes in energy and functional ability.
  • Report new or worsening fatigue.

Pain

Cancer-related pain may result from tumors, inflammation, surgery, nerve damage, or treatment effects.

Pain can interfere with sleep, movement, appetite, and emotional well-being.

CNA Considerations:

  • Ask about pain using the facility’s approved approach.
  • Observe verbal and nonverbal signs of discomfort.
  • Assist with positioning and comfort measures according to the care plan.
  • Report uncontrolled, new, or worsening pain.
  • Reassess comfort and communicate observations to nursing staff.

Do not independently change pain medication or treatment plans.

Nausea, Vomiting & Poor Appetite

Cancer or its treatments may affect appetite, taste, smell, swallowing, and digestion.

CNA Considerations:

  • Offer assistance with meals as appropriate.
  • Follow prescribed food textures, diet instructions, and fluid restrictions.
  • Provide oral hygiene and comfort measures.
  • Record intake and output when assigned.
  • Observe vomiting, poor intake, and dehydration signs.
  • Report persistent symptoms.

Mouth Sores (Mucositis)

Certain cancer treatments can cause inflammation or sores in the mouth and throat.

This may make eating, swallowing, or speaking painful.

CNA Considerations:

  • Assist with gentle oral care according to the care plan.
  • Report sores, bleeding, discomfort, or difficulty swallowing.
  • Avoid irritating tissues during mouth care.
  • Follow prescribed oral-care products and restrictions.

Hair Loss & Changes in Appearance

Some treatments cause hair loss, skin changes, swelling, scars, or body-image concerns.

CNA Considerations:

  • Ask about personal preferences for grooming.
  • Handle sensitive skin and scalp gently.
  • Respect privacy during bathing and dressing.
  • Do not assume appearance changes are emotionally distressing to every resident.
  • Offer support without making unsolicited comments about appearance.

Infection & Bleeding Precautions

Cancer and its treatments may affect bone marrow and immune function.

Hazardous Medication Exposure Precautions

Some cancer treatments involve hazardous medications. Drug residues may be present in a resident’s body fluids or waste for a period after treatment.

  • Follow facility-specific procedures for handling contaminated linens, body fluids, spills, and waste.
  • Use the PPE required by the medication-specific and facility protocols.
  • Ask the nurse when additional handling precautions are needed.
  • Do not assume every cancer treatment requires the same precautions.

Neutropenia

Neutropenia is an abnormally low neutrophil count, increasing vulnerability to infection.

  • Perform consistent hand hygiene.
  • Follow the resident’s prescribed infection-prevention measures.
  • Report fever, chills, new cough, or other signs of infection promptly.
  • Avoid exposing vulnerable residents to people with contagious illnesses according to facility guidance.

Important: Fever during neutropenia can indicate a life-threatening infection, even when other symptoms are mild.

Thrombocytopenia

Thrombocytopenia is a reduced platelet count, increasing bleeding risk.

  • Handle residents gently during transfers and personal care.
  • Observe unusual bruising, petechiae, or bleeding.
  • Follow prescribed bleeding precautions.
  • Report blood in urine or stool, nosebleeds, or bleeding gums.

Anemia

Anemia is a reduction in red blood cells or hemoglobin, potentially decreasing oxygen delivery.

Possible symptoms include:

  • Fatigue.
  • Weakness.
  • Dizziness.
  • Pallor.
  • Shortness of breath during activity.

Observe and report significant changes, and provide assistance with mobility when appropriate.

Mobility, Skin & Daily Living

Cancer or cancer treatment may cause weakness, pain, neuropathy, or reduced endurance.

CNA Considerations

  • Assess the level of assistance described in the care plan.
  • Encourage independence where safe.
  • Assist with walking and transfers using appropriate equipment.
  • Follow fall-prevention precautions.
  • Report new numbness, weakness, or balance changes.
  • Observe skin integrity, especially in residents with immobility, edema, or poor nutritional intake.
  • Follow prescribed precautions for surgical sites, radiation-treated skin, and medical devices.

Important: Do not independently apply topical products to radiation treatment areas, manipulate oncology devices, or perform procedures outside authorized CNA duties.

Emotional & Social Support

Residents with cancer may experience uncertainty, fear, frustration, sadness, or concerns about independence.

They may also feel hopeful, emotionally stable, or focused on ordinary daily activities.

Avoid assuming how a resident feels based solely on their diagnosis.

Therapeutic Communication

  • Listen without judgment.
  • Ask what matters most to the resident.
  • Respect preferences regarding discussion of illness.
  • Avoid unsolicited reassurance or predictions about outcomes.
  • Maintain normal social interaction when desired.
  • Support privacy and independence.
  • Report significant changes in mood, behavior, or coping.

Palliative Care

Palliative care is specialized care that focuses on relieving suffering and improving quality of life during serious illness.

It can be provided alongside surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and other cancer treatments.

Palliative care may address:

  • Pain and other physical symptoms.
  • Nausea, fatigue, and breathlessness.
  • Emotional and psychological distress.
  • Social and spiritual concerns.
  • Communication about goals of care.
  • Support for caregivers and family members.

Palliative Care Goals

  • Improve comfort.
  • Reduce symptom burden.
  • Preserve dignity and autonomy.
  • Support meaningful daily activities.
  • Align treatment and care with the person’s values.

Palliative Care vs. Hospice

Palliative CareHospice Care
Can begin at diagnosis of serious illnessTypically provided when a terminal illness is expected to be in its final months
May be given alongside curative treatmentFocuses on comfort rather than treatment intended to cure the terminal illness
Supports quality of life throughout illnessProvides comprehensive end-of-life support
Not limited to dying patientsIntended for people approaching the end of life

Important: Palliative care is not equivalent to giving up on treatment.

Supporting Residents Receiving Palliative or Hospice Care

  • Prioritize comfort and resident preferences.
  • Follow individualized positioning, hygiene, and mobility plans.
  • Observe and report pain, anxiety, agitation, breathlessness, and other symptoms.
  • Provide frequent gentle mouth care when appropriate.
  • Respect preferences for visitors, quiet, conversation, and spiritual support.
  • Avoid forcing food or fluids; follow the resident’s goals and care plan.
  • Support privacy, dignity, and family involvement as authorized.
  • Report uncontrolled symptoms promptly.

Do not independently decide to withhold or initiate treatment. Follow documented orders, the care plan, and nursing direction.

Patient Rights & Autonomy

Residents living with cancer retain their rights to:

  • Privacy and confidentiality.
  • Respectful, nondiscriminatory care.
  • Participation in care decisions.
  • Information about treatment options from qualified clinicians.
  • Consent to or refusal of treatment within applicable legal requirements.
  • Pain and symptom assessment.
  • Personal dignity and independence.
  • Appropriate family or support-person involvement according to their preferences and authorizations.

Advance Care Planning

Advance Directives: Legal documents that communicate healthcare wishes or identify someone authorized to make healthcare decisions when the person cannot.

Goals of Care: The treatments and outcomes that matter most to the resident, such as longevity, function, comfort, or time with family.

Code Status: Medical orders specifying resuscitation preferences.

CNA Role: Know where to obtain relevant care instructions, follow authorized orders and emergency procedures, and refer questions about treatment decisions to qualified staff.

When to Report

  • New or worsening pain.
  • Fever, chills, or signs of infection.
  • New bruising or bleeding.
  • Persistent nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
  • Poor oral intake or dehydration.
  • New mouth sores or swallowing difficulty.
  • Sudden or significant functional decline.
  • New confusion or behavioral changes.
  • New weakness, numbness, or difficulty walking.
  • Uncontrolled symptoms during palliative care.
  • Concerns about dignity, confidentiality, or treatment preferences.

Emergency signs: Severe breathing difficulty, uncontrolled bleeding, seizures, sudden neurological deficits, or unresponsiveness require immediate attention according to the resident’s orders, goals of care, and facility emergency protocols.

Clinical Connections

  • Therapeutic Communication: Listening and respecting preferences support the resident’s well-being.
  • Recognizing Patient Distress: Cancer complications and treatment effects may require urgent recognition.
  • Standard Precautions: Neutropenia and other conditions increase infection vulnerability.
  • Assisting with Feeding: Nausea, mucositis, and appetite changes may affect nutritional intake.
  • Fluid Balance: Vomiting, diarrhea, and poor intake can affect hydration.
  • Skin Integrity & Breakdown Prevention: Reduced mobility and treatment effects can increase skin vulnerability.
  • Fall Risk: Weakness, anemia, and neuropathy can increase fall risk.
  • Patient Rights: Comfort, dignity, privacy, and informed choices remain essential.
