Cancer

Cancer

Oct 26, 202612 min read

Overview

Cancer is a group of diseases in which abnormal cells grow and divide uncontrollably, invade surrounding tissues, and may spread to other parts of the body.

Cancer develops when genetic changes disrupt the normal mechanisms controlling cell growth, division, and death.

Unlike normal cells, cancer cells may:

  • Divide when they should not.
  • Ignore signals to stop growing.
  • Avoid programmed cell death (apoptosis).
  • Invade nearby tissues.
  • Stimulate the formation of new blood vessels.
  • Spread to distant locations.

Cancer can develop in almost any organ or tissue.

Important: Cancer is not a single disease. Different cancers have different causes, behaviors, treatment options, and prognoses.

Benign vs. Malignant Tumors

A tumor (neoplasm) is an abnormal mass of tissue. Tumors can be benign or malignant.

CharacteristicBenignMalignant
Cancerous?NoYes
GrowthOften slower, though variableMay grow rapidly or slowly
Invades nearby tissue?Does not invadeCan invade
Metastasizes?NoMay spread to distant locations

Important: A benign tumor can still be dangerous depending on its size and location. Some malignant cancers, such as leukemia, do not typically form solid tumors.

Metastasis

Metastasis is the spread of cancer cells from the original (primary) site to distant tissues or organs.

How Metastasis Occurs

  1. Cancer cells invade nearby tissues.
  2. Some cells enter blood vessels or lymphatic vessels.
  3. The cells travel through the bloodstream or lymphatic system.
  4. Some leave the vessels and enter distant tissues.
  5. They may multiply and form secondary tumors.

Common Sites of Metastasis

  • Bones: May cause pain or fractures.
  • Lungs: May cause coughing or difficulty breathing.
  • Liver: May affect liver function and sometimes cause jaundice.
  • Brain: May cause headaches, seizures, weakness, or neurological changes.

The sites affected depend on the primary cancer.

Important: Metastatic cancer retains the identity of the original cancer.

For example, breast cancer that spreads to the lungs is called metastatic breast cancer, not primary lung cancer.

Types of Cancer

Cancers can be classified according to the cells or tissues where they originate.

TypeTissue of OriginExamples
CarcinomaEpithelial cells lining organs and body surfacesLung cancer, many breast cancers, colorectal cancer, prostate cancer
SarcomaBone, muscle, fat, cartilage, and other connective tissuesOsteosarcoma, liposarcoma

LymphomaLymphocytes within lymphatic and other tissuesHodgkin lymphoma, non-Hodgkin lymphoma
Central Nervous System (CNS) CancersCells and tissues of the brain or spinal cordGlioblastoma, medulloblastoma
MyelomaPlasma cells, a type of immune cellMultiple myeloma
MelanomaMelanocytes, the cells that produce melaninCutaneous melanoma

Important Distinctions

  • Carcinoma: Cancer originating in epithelial tissue; the most common broad cancer category.
  • Sarcoma: Cancer of connective or supportive tissues.
  • Leukemia: Cancer typically involving blood-forming tissue and abnormal blood cells.
  • Lymphoma: Cancer originating in lymphocytes.
  • CNS Cancers: Cancers originating in the brain or spinal cord; not all brain tumors are malignant.
  • Melanoma: Cancer of pigment-producing cells; often occurs in the skin.

Important: Many cancers cause few or no symptoms in their early stages. Symptoms alone do not establish a cancer diagnosis.

Causes & Risk Factors

Cancer arises from genetic changes affecting normal cell behavior.

These changes may result from:

  • Random errors during cell division.
  • Inherited genetic variants.
  • Environmental or occupational exposures.
  • Certain infections.
  • The accumulation of changes over time.

Major Risk Factors

Risk FactorHow It ContributesExamples
Tobacco UseExposure to chemicals that damage DNALung, bladder, pancreatic, head and neck cancers
Ultraviolet (UV) RadiationDamages DNA in skin cellsMelanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma

A risk factor increases the likelihood of developing cancer but does not guarantee that cancer will occur.

Carcinogens

A carcinogen is an agent capable of causing cancer.

Carcinogens may be chemical, physical, or biological.

CategoryExamples
ChemicalTobacco smoke, asbestos, benzene
PhysicalUltraviolet radiation, ionizing radiation
BiologicalHigh-risk HPV, hepatitis B and C viruses, Helicobacter pylori

Important: Carcinogen exposure does not guarantee cancer. Risk depends on the agent, exposure, duration, and individual susceptibility.

Cancer Prevention

Measures that can reduce the risk of certain cancers include:

  • Avoiding tobacco and secondhand smoke.
  • Limiting alcohol consumption.
  • Protecting skin from excessive UV exposure.
  • Maintaining regular physical activity.
  • Following a healthy dietary pattern.
  • Receiving recommended HPV and hepatitis B vaccinations.
  • Reducing avoidable occupational carcinogen exposure.
  • Participating in age- and risk-appropriate cancer screening.

Cancer cannot always be prevented, and having cancer does not imply that someone made unhealthy choices.

How Cancer Is Diagnosed

Cancer diagnosis often involves a combination of clinical evaluation, laboratory testing, imaging, and tissue examination.

MethodPurpose
Medical History & Physical ExaminationIdentify symptoms, findings, and risk factors
Screening TestsDetect possible cancer or precancerous changes before symptoms develop
Blood & Laboratory TestsIdentify abnormalities or measure selected biomarkers
Imaging (CT, MRI, Ultrasound, PET)Locate abnormalities and assess their extent
EndoscopyExamine internal organs and potentially obtain tissue samples
BiopsyRemove cells or tissue for microscopic examination
PathologyIdentify cancer type, cellular features, and other characteristics
Molecular / Biomarker TestingIdentify tumor characteristics that may guide treatment

Biopsy

A biopsy removes a tissue or cell sample for examination.

A pathologist examines the sample to determine whether cancer is present and may identify the type and grade.

A biopsy is often needed for a definitive diagnosis, although some cancers can be diagnosed through specialized blood or bone marrow testing.

Screening vs. Diagnosis

  • Screening: Tests people without symptoms to look for possible disease.
  • Diagnostic Testing: Investigates symptoms or abnormal screening results to establish a diagnosis.

A positive screening result does not automatically mean cancer is present.

Staging Cancer

Cancer staging describes the extent of cancer within the body, including tumor size, involvement of nearby structures or lymph nodes, and distant spread.

Staging helps determine treatment options and estimate prognosis.

TNM Staging System

The TNM system is used for many solid tumors.

  • T — Tumor: Size or local extent of the primary tumor.
  • N — Nodes: Whether nearby regional lymph nodes contain cancer.
  • M — Metastasis: Whether cancer has spread to distant sites.

General Cancer Stages

StageGeneral Meaning
Stage 0Carcinoma in situ: abnormal cells have not invaded nearby tissue
Stage IOften a relatively small or localized cancer
Stage IIMore extensive local disease, depending on cancer type
Stage IIIMore advanced local or regional disease, often involving nearby nodes
Stage IVDistant metastatic spread for many solid cancers

Important: These descriptions are general. Staging differs by cancer type; leukemia, lymphoma, and many CNS tumors use other classification or staging approaches.

Stage vs. Grade

  • Stage: How far the cancer has spread.
  • Grade: How abnormal the cancer cells appear and, in some cancers, how aggressively they may behave.

Stage describes extent; grade describes cellular characteristics.

Treatment Options

Cancer treatment depends on cancer type, stage, molecular characteristics, overall health, and the patient’s goals.

TreatmentHow It Works
SurgeryRemoves cancerous tissue; may also relieve symptoms
ChemotherapyUses drugs to kill or inhibit cancer cells, often affecting other rapidly dividing cells
Radiation TherapyUses high-energy radiation to damage or destroy cancer cells
ImmunotherapyHelps the immune system recognize or attack cancer
Targeted TherapyTargets specific molecules or pathways involved in cancer growth
Hormone TherapyReduces or blocks hormones that support certain cancers
Stem Cell TransplantRestores blood-forming cells after certain intensive treatments
Active SurveillanceCarefully monitors selected cancers without immediate treatment
Palliative TreatmentsRelieve symptoms or complications, sometimes alongside cancer-directed treatment

Treatment may combine several approaches.

Treatment Goals

  • Curative: Attempt to eliminate the cancer.
  • Disease Control: Slow progression or maintain remission.
  • Palliative: Relieve symptoms and improve quality of life.

Not every cancer is curable, but even incurable cancers may be treatable.

Palliative Care

Palliative care is specialized care focused on relieving symptoms, reducing suffering, and improving quality of life for people living with serious illness.

It can address:

  • Pain.
  • Fatigue.
  • Nausea and other physical symptoms.
  • Emotional distress.
  • Social and spiritual concerns.
  • Treatment decisions and goals of care.

Palliative care can begin at diagnosis and can be provided alongside treatments intended to cure or control cancer.

Palliative Care vs. Hospice

  • Palliative Care: Can be provided at any stage of serious illness, including during curative treatment.
  • Hospice Care: Focuses on comfort and quality of life when care goals have shifted away from curing the terminal illness.

See Caring for Residents with Cancer.

CNA Considerations

  • Observe and report new symptoms or changes from baseline.
  • Follow prescribed infection-prevention precautions.
  • Report fever or signs of infection promptly, especially in residents receiving chemotherapy.
  • Observe fatigue, pain, food and fluid intake, nausea, bowel changes, and skin integrity.
  • Support permitted activity and independence.
  • Follow individualized mobility, nutritional, and treatment-related precautions.
  • Protect confidentiality and respect the resident’s care preferences.
  • Do not independently diagnose cancer, interpret staging, or provide cancer-specific treatment.

When to Report

  • New or worsening pain.
  • Fever, chills, or signs of infection.
  • Unexpected bruising or bleeding.
  • Significant fatigue or weakness.
  • Persistent nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
  • Poor intake, dehydration, or rapid weight loss.
  • New confusion or functional decline.
  • New difficulty breathing or swallowing.
  • Changes around surgical wounds or treatment sites.

Emergency signs: Severe breathing difficulty, uncontrolled bleeding, sudden neurological changes, or unresponsiveness require immediate emergency response.

A fever in a patient with known or suspected neutropenia may represent a medical emergency and requires immediate clinical assessment.
