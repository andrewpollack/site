Cardiovascular System

Cardiovascular System

Oct 26, 202610 min read

Overview

The cardiovascular system circulates blood throughout the body to deliver oxygen and nutrients, remove carbon dioxide and other waste products, and maintain blood pressure and tissue perfusion.

It consists of the heart, blood vessels, and blood.

The right side of the heart pumps blood to the lungs, while the left side pumps oxygen-rich blood throughout the body.

Major Structures

Heart

The heart is a muscular organ containing four chambers and four valves that maintain one-way blood flow.

  • Right Atrium: Receives oxygen-poor blood returning from the body.
  • Right Ventricle: Pumps oxygen-poor blood to the lungs through the pulmonary arteries.
  • Left Atrium: Receives oxygen-rich blood returning from the lungs.
  • Left Ventricle: Pumps oxygen-rich blood to the body through the aorta.
  • Heart Valves: Prevent backward blood flow.
    • Tricuspid Valve: Right atrium → right ventricle.
    • Pulmonary Valve: Right ventricle → pulmonary artery.
    • Mitral (Bicuspid) Valve: Left atrium → left ventricle.
    • Aortic Valve: Left ventricle → aorta.
  • Coronary Arteries: Supply oxygen-rich blood to the heart muscle itself.

Blood Vessels

  • Arteries: Carry blood away from the heart, generally under higher pressure.
  • Arterioles: Small arteries that regulate blood flow into capillary networks and contribute to vascular resistance.
  • Capillaries: Microscopic vessels connecting arterioles and venules, allowing exchange of gases, nutrients, and waste products.
  • Venules: Small vessels that collect blood from capillaries and drain into larger veins.
  • Veins: Return blood toward the heart, often containing valves that prevent backward flow, particularly in the limbs.

Important: Arteries and veins are defined by the direction of blood flow, not oxygen content. Pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood, while pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood.

Blood

Blood is a specialized connective tissue consisting of plasma and formed elements.

  • Red Blood Cells (Erythrocytes): Transport oxygen using hemoglobin and carry some carbon dioxide.
  • White Blood Cells (Leukocytes): Support immune defense and fight infection.
  • Platelets (Thrombocytes): Help form blood clots to stop bleeding.
  • Plasma: Liquid portion of blood, mostly water, containing proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, and waste products.

Key Processes

  • Pulmonary Circulation: Blood travels from the right side of the heart to the lungs for gas exchange, then returns to the left side.

  • Systemic Circulation: Oxygen-rich blood travels from the left side of the heart to the body, and oxygen-poor blood returns to the right side.

  • Cardiac Cycle: Repeated contraction and relaxation of the heart.

    • Systole: Ventricles contract and eject blood.
    • Diastole: Ventricles relax and fill with blood.

  • Heart Rate: Number of heartbeats per minute.

  • Stroke Volume: Amount of blood pumped by one ventricle with each heartbeat.

  • Cardiac Output: Volume of blood pumped by one ventricle per minute.

    Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume

  • Blood Pressure: Force exerted by circulating blood against vessel walls, typically measured in the arteries.

  • Systemic Vascular Resistance: Resistance to blood flow through the systemic circulation, influenced strongly by arteriolar diameter.

  • Tissue Perfusion: Delivery of blood to tissues to supply oxygen and nutrients.

  • Hemostasis: Process of stopping bleeding through vessel responses, platelet activity, and coagulation.

Effects of Aging

  • Increased Arterial Stiffness: Large arteries become less elastic, contributing to higher systolic blood pressure.
  • Reduced Cardiac Reserve: The heart may be less able to increase its pumping performance during exertion or illness.
  • Changes in Heart Muscle: Heart walls may thicken, and ventricular relaxation and filling may become less efficient.
  • Changes in Heart Valves: Valves may thicken or stiffen, increasing the likelihood of valve dysfunction.
  • Reduced Blood Pressure Regulation: Less sensitive pressure receptors can contribute to dizziness or Orthostatic Hypotension when changing positions.
  • Increased Susceptibility to Arrhythmias: Changes in the heart’s electrical conduction system can increase the risk of abnormal heart rhythms.

Clinical Implications

  • Increased vulnerability to cardiovascular disease.
  • Greater risk of dizziness and falls when changing positions.
  • Reduced exercise tolerance and increased fatigue during activity.
  • Greater importance of monitoring blood pressure, pulse, and changes from baseline.
  • Observe swelling, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, and reduced activity tolerance.

Important: Anemia, markedly reduced white blood cell counts, uncontrolled hypertension, and heart failure are not inevitable consequences of normal aging. New abnormalities require evaluation.

Important Distinctions

  • Arteriosclerosis vs. Atherosclerosis: Arteriosclerosis is a broad term for arterial thickening or hardening. Atherosclerosis specifically involves plaque buildup within artery walls and is one form of arteriosclerosis.
  • Angina vs. Myocardial Infarction: Angina is a symptom of temporary myocardial ischemia, typically without heart muscle death. Myocardial infarction involves heart muscle injury or death caused by prolonged ischemia. New chest discomfort requires prompt evaluation; CNAs should not attempt to distinguish these conditions independently.
  • Peripheral Artery Disease vs. Chronic Venous Insufficiency: PAD reduces arterial blood delivery to tissues and can produce cool extremities or arterial ulcers. Chronic venous insufficiency impairs blood return and can cause edema, skin discoloration, and venous stasis ulcers.
  • Heart Failure vs. Heart Attack: Heart failure involves impaired pumping or filling of the heart. A heart attack causes acute heart muscle injury due to insufficient blood supply. A heart attack can lead to heart failure.

When to Report

  • Blood pressure or pulse outside prescribed parameters.
  • New dizziness, weakness, or fainting.
  • New or worsening swelling of the legs or feet.
  • Unusual shortness of breath, fatigue, or reduced activity tolerance.
  • New irregular pulse or palpitations.
  • Changes in skin color, temperature, or circulation.
  • Unexplained bruising or bleeding.
  • Sudden weight gain in a resident being monitored for fluid retention.

Emergency signs: New chest pain or pressure, severe breathing difficulty, sudden one-sided weakness, loss of consciousness, or sudden severe chest, back, or abdominal pain require immediate emergency response.

Clinical Connections

  • Hypertension: Persistent elevation of arterial blood pressure can increase cardiovascular risk.
  • Hypotension: Low blood pressure may reduce tissue perfusion.
  • Orthostatic Hypotension: Postural blood pressure changes can contribute to dizziness and falls.
  • Blood Loss: Reduced circulating blood volume can impair tissue perfusion.
  • Dehydration: Fluid loss can contribute to low blood pressure and an increased heart rate.
  • Vital Signs: Blood pressure and pulse provide important cardiovascular observations.
  • Fall Risk: Dizziness, fainting, and impaired circulation can increase fall risk.
  • Recognizing Patient Distress: Chest discomfort and sudden circulation changes may signal emergencies.
