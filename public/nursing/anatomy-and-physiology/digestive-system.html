Digestive System

Digestive System

Oct 26, 202611 min read

Overview

The digestive system (gastrointestinal system) breaks down food, absorbs nutrients and water, and eliminates undigested waste.

It provides the body with nutrients needed for energy, growth, tissue repair, and normal cellular function.

The digestive system consists of two major groups:

  • Alimentary Canal (Gastrointestinal Tract): Continuous tube through which food passes, from the mouth to the anus.
  • Accessory Digestive Organs: Structures that assist digestion, including the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder, and pancreas.

Major Structures

Alimentary Canal

  • Mouth (Oral Cavity): Begins digestion through chewing and mixing food with saliva.
    • Teeth: Mechanically break food into smaller pieces.
    • Tongue: Moves food during chewing and forms a bolus for swallowing.
    • Salivary Glands: Produce saliva containing digestive enzymes, including salivary amylase.
  • Pharynx (Throat): Passageway for swallowed food and liquids toward the esophagus.
  • Esophagus: Muscular tube transporting food from the pharynx to the stomach through peristalsis.
  • Stomach: Muscular organ that stores, mixes, and partially digests food using gastric acid and enzymes, producing chyme.
  • Small Intestine: Primary site of chemical digestion and nutrient absorption.
    • Duodenum: First segment; receives chyme, bile, and pancreatic digestive secretions.
    • Jejunum: Middle segment; major site of nutrient absorption.
    • Ileum: Final segment; absorbs remaining nutrients, including vitamin B12 and bile salts.
  • Large Intestine (Colon): Absorbs water and electrolytes and forms feces.
    • Cecum: First portion of the large intestine.
    • Ascending Colon: Travels upward along the right side of the abdomen.
    • Transverse Colon: Crosses the upper abdomen.
    • Descending Colon: Travels downward along the left side.
    • Sigmoid Colon: S-shaped portion leading to the rectum.
  • Rectum: Stores feces before elimination.
  • Anus: Opening through which feces leave the body, controlled by anal sphincters.

Accessory Digestive Organs

  • Liver: Large organ in the upper right abdomen.
    • Produces bile to assist fat digestion.
    • Processes absorbed nutrients.
    • Stores glycogen, vitamins, and minerals.
    • Metabolizes medications and toxins.
    • Produces important blood proteins.
  • Gallbladder: Stores and concentrates bile produced by the liver, releasing it into the small intestine during digestion.
  • Pancreas: Organ behind the stomach with digestive and endocrine functions.
    • Exocrine Function: Produces digestive enzymes and bicarbonate delivered into the duodenum.
    • Endocrine Function: Produces insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose.

Intestinal Lining

  • Villi: Fingerlike projections in the small intestine that increase surface area for nutrient absorption.
  • Microvilli: Microscopic projections on intestinal epithelial cells that further increase absorptive surface area.
  • Intestinal Capillaries: Absorb many nutrients into the bloodstream.
  • Lacteals: Specialized lymphatic vessels within villi that absorb dietary fats.

Important: Villi are characteristic of the small intestine, not the entire digestive tract.

Key Processes

Digestion

Digestion involves mechanically and chemically breaking food into smaller components that can be absorbed.

  • Mastication: Chewing food to break it into smaller pieces.
  • Salivation: Saliva moistens food and begins starch digestion.
  • Swallowing (Deglutition): Coordinated movement of food from the mouth through the pharynx into the esophagus.
  • Airway Protection: The epiglottis and other swallowing mechanisms help protect the airway.
  • Peristalsis: Coordinated, wave-like smooth muscle contractions that move material through the digestive tract.
  • Mechanical Digestion: Physical breakdown and mixing of food.
  • Chemical Digestion: Enzymes and digestive secretions break nutrients into absorbable components.

Pathway of Food Through the Digestive Tract

  1. Mouth: Food is chewed, mixed with saliva, and formed into a bolus.
  2. Pharynx: Swallowing moves the bolus toward the esophagus.
  3. Esophagus: Peristalsis transports food into the stomach.
  4. Stomach: Gastric acid and enzymes begin significant protein digestion; muscular contractions produce chyme.
  5. Small Intestine: Bile and pancreatic enzymes assist digestion; most nutrients are absorbed.
  6. Large Intestine: Water and electrolytes are absorbed, and feces are formed.
  7. Rectum: Stores feces before defecation.
  8. Anus: Feces are eliminated during a bowel movement.

Nutrient Absorption

  • Carbohydrates: Broken down into simple sugars, such as glucose.
  • Proteins: Broken down into amino acids and small peptides.
  • Fats: Bile helps emulsify fats, allowing digestive enzymes to break them down for absorption.
  • Vitamins and Minerals: Absorbed primarily in the small intestine, with specific absorption sites depending on the nutrient.
  • Water: Absorbed throughout the intestines, predominantly in the small intestine, with additional absorption in the colon.

Elimination

  • Feces Formation: Undigested material, bacteria, water, and other substances form stool.
  • Defecation: Coordinated contractions and relaxation of the rectum and anal sphincters allow stool to leave the body.
  • Mucus Secretion: Helps lubricate intestinal contents and protect the intestinal lining.

Effects of Aging

  • Changes in Oral Health: Tooth loss, reduced chewing efficiency, dry mouth, and dental problems may interfere with eating.
  • Changes in Swallowing: Some age-related changes in swallowing physiology may reduce swallowing reserve, especially during illness.
  • Changes in Gastrointestinal Motility: Motility patterns may change, but significant slowing and constipation are not inevitable consequences of aging.
  • Increased Constipation Risk: Reduced mobility, inadequate fluid or fiber intake, and medication effects can make constipation more likely.
  • Changes in Liver Physiology: Liver size and blood flow may decrease with age, potentially affecting the metabolism of certain medications.
  • Changes in Appetite: Taste, smell, medications, illness, and social factors can influence food intake.

Clinical Implications

  • Increased vulnerability to poor nutritional intake.
  • Dental problems and swallowing difficulties may affect safe eating.
  • Reduced mobility and certain medications can increase constipation risk.
  • Dehydration may worsen constipation.
  • Medication effects may be more pronounced because of changes in metabolism.
  • Greater importance of observing food intake, swallowing, bowel patterns, and changes from baseline.

Important: Persistent constipation, unexplained weight loss, difficulty swallowing, gastrointestinal bleeding, and significantly impaired digestion are not normal consequences of aging and require evaluation.

Important Distinctions

  • Constipation vs. Fecal Impaction: Constipation involves difficult or infrequent bowel movements; impaction involves stool that has become lodged and cannot be passed normally.
  • GERD vs. Peptic Ulcer Disease: GERD involves reflux into the esophagus; ulcers involve open sores in the stomach or duodenal lining.
  • Diverticulosis vs. Diverticulitis: Diverticulosis means diverticula are present; diverticulitis means they have become inflamed.
  • Gallstones vs. Cholecystitis: Gallstones are hardened deposits in the gallbladder or bile ducts; cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder, often due to gallstone obstruction.
  • Diarrhea vs. Overflow Diarrhea: Liquid stool may leak around a fecal impaction, so loose stool does not always mean constipation is absent.

Important: CNAs observe and report gastrointestinal abnormalities rather than independently diagnosing conditions, performing disimpaction, or administering bowel treatments without appropriate authorization.

When to Report

  • New or persistent constipation or diarrhea.
  • Significant changes in bowel movement frequency, appearance, or consistency.
  • New abdominal pain, swelling, or distention.
  • Nausea, vomiting, or inability to tolerate food or fluids.
  • Difficulty chewing or swallowing.
  • Coughing, choking, or a wet-sounding voice during meals.
  • Blood in stool or unusually dark, tarry stools.
  • Poor appetite, unexpected weight loss, or decreased fluid intake.
  • New bowel incontinence or suspected fecal impaction.
  • Signs of dehydration.

Emergency signs: Severe sudden abdominal pain, vomiting blood, substantial gastrointestinal bleeding, inability to breathe from choking, or signs of shock require immediate emergency response.

Clinical Connections

  • Assisting with Feeding: Proper meal assistance supports nutrition and safe swallowing.
  • Dysphagia & Aspiration Precautions: Swallowing problems can increase choking and aspiration risk.
  • Elimination Care: Bowel patterns and continence are important aspects of resident care.
  • Constipation: Mobility, fluids, diet, and medications influence bowel function.
  • Dehydration: Vomiting, diarrhea, and poor fluid intake can disturb fluid balance.
  • Hand Hygiene: Certain gastrointestinal infections can spread through contaminated hands and surfaces.
  • Vital Signs: Changes in temperature, pulse, and blood pressure may accompany dehydration, bleeding, or infection.
  • Patient Positions: Appropriate positioning supports comfort, feeding, and aspiration precautions.
