Musculoskeletal System

Musculoskeletal System

Oct 26, 20267 min read

Overview

The musculoskeletal system provides structural support, protects internal organs, maintains posture, and enables movement.

It consists of bones, skeletal muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, cartilage, and other connective tissues.

The system also supports mineral storage, blood cell production, and heat generation.

Major Structures

Skeletal System

  • Bones: Provide support, protect organs, store minerals such as calcium and phosphorus, and contain bone marrow.
    • Axial Skeleton: Skull, vertebral column, ribs, and sternum. Supports the body’s central axis and protects vital organs.
    • Appendicular Skeleton: Arms, legs, shoulders, and pelvic girdle. Supports movement and locomotion.
  • Joints: Connections between bones that provide stability and, depending on the joint, permit movement.
  • Tendons: Connect muscles to bones, transmitting force to produce movement.
  • Ligaments: Connect bones to other bones, helping stabilize joints.
  • Cartilage: Flexible connective tissue that cushions joints and supports structures such as the rib cage.
  • Bone Marrow: Tissue within bones. Red marrow produces blood cells; yellow marrow primarily stores fat.

Muscular System

  • Skeletal Muscle: Attached to bones through tendons. Produces voluntary movement and maintains posture.
  • Smooth Muscle: Involuntary muscle found in structures such as blood vessels, the digestive tract, and bladder.
  • Cardiac Muscle: Specialized involuntary muscle that contracts to pump blood through the heart.

Vertebral Column

The spine supports the body, protects the spinal cord, and allows movement.

Intervertebral Discs: Fibrocartilaginous structures between most adjacent movable vertebrae that absorb shock and permit movement.

Other Important Structures

  • Temporomandibular Joint (TMJ): Connects the mandible (lower jaw) to the temporal bone of the skull, allowing chewing and speaking.
  • Pelvis: Connects the spine to the lower limbs, supports body weight, and protects pelvic organs.
  • Hip Joint: Ball-and-socket joint connecting the femur to the pelvis.

Key Processes

  • Muscle Contraction: Produces movement, maintains posture, and stabilizes joints.
  • Joint Movement: Includes flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation.
  • Weight Bearing: Bones, muscles, and joints support body weight during standing, walking, and transfers.
  • Bone Remodeling: Continuous breakdown and replacement of bone tissue.
  • Thermoregulation: Muscle activity generates heat; shivering produces heat through repeated involuntary contractions.
  • Blood Cell Production (Hematopoiesis): Red bone marrow produces red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.
  • Mineral Storage: Bones store calcium and phosphorus and help regulate their availability.
  • Support of Elimination: Skeletal and smooth muscles help control urination and defecation.

Effects of Aging

  • Reduced Bone Density: Bone mass may decline, increasing vulnerability to fractures.
  • Reduced Muscle Mass (Sarcopenia): Loss of muscle mass and strength can impair mobility and balance.
  • Reduced Flexibility: Changes in connective tissues and joints may limit range of motion.
  • Slower Muscle Responses: Changes in muscle and nervous-system function can affect coordination and reaction time.
  • Reduced Physical Endurance: Fatigue may occur more easily during prolonged activity.

Clinical Implications

  • Increased risk of falls and fractures.
  • Greater difficulty with transfers, walking, and ADLs.
  • Increased vulnerability to deconditioning during prolonged bed rest.
  • Greater importance of appropriate mobility, exercise, and restorative care.
  • Encourage independence while following individualized mobility restrictions.

Important: Osteoporosis, severe weakness, and inability to walk are not inevitable consequences of aging. New functional decline should be reported.

Important Distinctions

  • Osteoarthritis vs. Rheumatoid Arthritis: Osteoarthritis involves degenerative changes affecting cartilage and other joint tissues. Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune inflammatory disease that can affect multiple joints and other organs.
  • Sprain vs. Strain: A sprain involves ligaments; a strain involves muscles or tendons.
  • Osteopenia vs. Osteoporosis: Osteopenia means bone mineral density is lower than normal; osteoporosis involves more substantial skeletal fragility and increased fracture risk.
  • Muscle Atrophy vs. Contracture: Atrophy is loss of muscle mass. A contracture is persistent shortening or tightening of tissues that restricts joint movement.

When to Report

  • New or worsening joint or muscle pain.
  • Sudden difficulty walking, standing, or transferring.
  • Swelling, bruising, or visible deformity after an injury.
  • Reduced range of motion or increasing stiffness.
  • New weakness or unexpected functional decline.
  • Signs of injury following a fall.

Suspected fractures, sudden severe pain after injury, or new one-sided weakness require urgent evaluation. Follow emergency-response procedures when indicated.

Clinical Connections

  • Limited Mobility: Reduced mobility can lead to weakness, contractures, and other complications.
  • Patient Positions: Proper alignment supports comfort, joint positioning, and pressure prevention.
  • Range-of-Motion Exercises: Prescribed exercises help maintain joint mobility.
  • Assisting with Ambulation: Safe walking assistance promotes mobility and independence.
  • Safe Patient Transfers: Proper transfer techniques reduce injury risk.
  • Body Mechanics & Injury Prevention: Safe movement protects residents and caregivers.
  • Restorative & Rehabilitative Care: Individualized activities help maintain or restore function.
  • Fall Risk: Weakness, balance changes, and reduced mobility can increase fall risk.
