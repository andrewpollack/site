Urinary System

Urinary System

Oct 26, 202611 min read

Overview

The urinary system (renal system) filters blood, removes waste products and excess water, regulates fluid and electrolyte balance, and produces urine.

It consists of the kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, and urethra.

The kidneys also help:

  • Regulate Blood Pressure: Adjust blood volume and release renin, which participates in blood pressure regulation.
  • Stimulate Red Blood Cell Production: The kidneys release erythropoietin (EPO), which signals the bone marrow to produce red blood cells.
  • Support Calcium Absorption: Activate vitamin D, helping the intestines absorb calcium.
  • Maintain Acid-Base Balance: Remove excess acid and regulate bicarbonate levels.

The bladder stores urine until signals from the nervous system coordinate urination.

Major Structures

Upper Urinary Tract

  • Kidneys: Two bean-shaped organs located toward the back of the abdomen, on either side of the spine, below the rib cage.
    • Renal Cortex: Outer region containing parts of the structures that filter blood.
    • Renal Medulla: Inner region containing structures involved in concentrating urine.
    • Nephrons: Functional units of the kidneys that filter blood and form urine.
      • Glomerulus: Network of capillaries that filters fluid and small substances from blood.
      • Renal Tubules: Reabsorb needed substances and secrete additional wastes into the forming urine.
    • Renal Pelvis: Collects urine and channels it into the ureter.
  • Ureters: Two muscular tubes that transport urine from the kidneys to the bladder through peristalsis.

Lower Urinary Tract

  • Urinary Bladder: Hollow, muscular organ in the pelvis that stores urine.
    • Detrusor Muscle: Smooth muscle in the bladder wall that contracts during urination.
    • Stretch Receptors: Detect bladder filling and send signals to the nervous system.
  • Urethra: Tube that carries urine from the bladder to the outside of the body.
  • Internal Urethral Sphincter: Involuntary smooth muscle at the bladder neck that helps prevent urine leakage.
  • External Urethral Sphincter: Skeletal muscle under voluntary control that helps maintain continence.

Supporting Structures

  • Pelvic Floor Muscles: Help support the bladder and maintain urinary continence.
  • Renal Arteries: Supply blood to the kidneys for filtration.
  • Renal Veins: Return blood from the kidneys to circulation.

Urine Pathway: Kidneys → Ureters → Bladder → Urethra → Outside the body.

Key Processes

Urine Formation

Urine is produced through three primary processes:

  1. Glomerular Filtration: Water and small dissolved substances move from blood into the nephron.
  2. Tubular Reabsorption: Needed water, glucose, electrolytes, and other substances return to the bloodstream.
  3. Tubular Secretion: Additional wastes, medications, and excess ions move from the blood into the renal tubules.

Remaining fluid and dissolved waste products become urine.

Urination (Micturition)

Urination requires coordination between the bladder, sphincters, spinal cord, and brain.

  1. The kidneys continuously produce urine.
  2. Urine travels through the ureters and collects in the bladder.
  3. As the bladder fills, stretch receptors send signals to the nervous system.
  4. The person becomes aware of the need to urinate.
  5. During normal voluntary urination, the detrusor muscle contracts and urethral sphincters relax.
  6. Urine flows through the urethra and exits the body.

Continence: Ability to control when and where urination occurs.

Incontinence: Involuntary urine leakage caused by impaired bladder storage, sphincter function, neurological control, or other factors.

Fluid & Electrolyte Regulation

  • Water Balance: Kidneys adjust the amount of water excreted in urine.
  • Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH): Increases water reabsorption, helping conserve body fluids.
  • Aldosterone: Promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion.
  • Electrolyte Balance: Kidneys regulate sodium, potassium, calcium, and other electrolytes.
  • Acid-Base Balance: Kidneys help maintain blood pH by excreting acid and regulating bicarbonate.

Blood Pressure & Hormone Regulation

  • Renin: Released by the kidneys and activates the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), which helps regulate blood pressure.
  • Erythropoietin (EPO): Stimulates bone marrow to produce red blood cells.
  • Vitamin D Activation: Kidneys convert vitamin D into its active hormonal form, calcitriol, which supports calcium absorption.

Effects of Aging

  • Reduced Kidney Reserve: Kidney blood flow and filtration capacity may decline, reducing the ability to handle physiological stress.
  • Altered Medication Clearance: Reduced kidney function can cause certain medications to accumulate.
  • Reduced Urine-Concentrating Ability: Kidneys may become less effective at conserving water during dehydration.
  • Reduced Bladder Capacity: Some older adults experience decreased functional bladder capacity and more frequent urination.
  • Bladder Emptying Changes: Incomplete emptying may become more common, increasing the risk of urinary retention and infection.
  • Pelvic Floor Changes: Reduced muscle support may contribute to urinary leakage.
  • Changes in Prostate Size: Prostate enlargement in some older men can obstruct urine flow.

Clinical Implications

  • Increased vulnerability to dehydration and electrolyte disturbances.
  • Changes in urinary frequency, urgency, or nighttime urination.
  • Increased risk of urinary retention and urinary tract infections in some residents.
  • Greater sensitivity to medications cleared through the kidneys.
  • Increased need for appropriate toileting assistance and observation of urinary patterns.

Important: Urinary incontinence, kidney failure, and urinary tract infections are not inevitable consequences of aging.

Important Distinctions

  • AKI vs. CKD: Acute kidney injury develops suddenly; chronic kidney disease develops or persists over months or years.
  • Urinary Retention vs. Incontinence: Retention is inadequate bladder emptying; incontinence is involuntary leakage. Both can occur together.
  • Stress vs. Urge Incontinence: Stress leakage occurs with increased abdominal pressure; urge leakage follows a sudden compelling need to urinate.
  • UTI vs. Kidney Infection: A bladder infection affects the lower urinary tract; pyelonephritis affects the kidney and may cause more serious illness.
  • Oliguria vs. Anuria: Oliguria means abnormally low urine output; anuria means nearly absent urine output.

Important: Cloudy or foul-smelling urine alone does not establish a UTI, and a positive urine culture does not always mean a symptomatic infection. CNAs report objective findings rather than diagnosing infection.

CNAs do not independently diagnose urinary conditions, prescribe fluid restrictions, or perform urinary catheter procedures unless specifically authorized within their role and training.

When to Report

  • New burning, pain, urgency, or difficulty with urination.
  • New or worsening urinary incontinence.
  • Inability to urinate or suspected urinary retention.
  • Markedly decreased urine output or significant change from baseline.
  • Blood in the urine.
  • Fever, chills, flank pain, or suspected infection.
  • New lower abdominal pain or distention.
  • Unexpected changes in intake and output.
  • New swelling, dizziness, or signs of dehydration.
  • Sudden confusion or functional decline, particularly when accompanied by other signs of illness.

Emergency signs: Inability to urinate with severe pain or distention, severe flank pain with fever, sudden unresponsiveness, or signs of severe infection require urgent or emergency evaluation according to facility protocol.

Clinical Connections

  • Elimination Care: Proper toileting support and observation help maintain comfort and dignity.
  • Incontinence Care: Prompt hygiene reduces moisture exposure and skin breakdown.
  • Fluid Balance: Kidneys regulate water and electrolyte levels.
  • Dehydration: Reduced fluid intake or excessive fluid loss can affect kidney function.
  • Skin Integrity & Breakdown Prevention: Urinary incontinence can contribute to moisture-associated skin damage.
  • Vital Signs: Fever, blood pressure changes, and pulse changes may accompany urinary illness.
  • Standard Precautions: Urinary infections and catheter care require appropriate infection-prevention practices.
  • Recognizing Patient Distress: Acute urinary retention or severe infection may require urgent attention.
