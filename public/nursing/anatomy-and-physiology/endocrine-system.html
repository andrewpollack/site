Endocrine System

Endocrine System

Oct 26, 202610 min read

Overview

The endocrine system is a network of glands and organs that produce and release hormones, chemical messengers that regulate body functions.

Hormones generally travel through the bloodstream and act on target cells containing the appropriate receptors.

The endocrine system helps regulate:

  • Metabolism and energy use.
  • Growth and development.
  • Blood glucose levels.
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance.
  • Stress responses.
  • Reproduction and sexual function.
  • Sleep, mood, and other aspects of homeostasis.

The endocrine system works closely with the Nervous System. Nervous-system signals are often rapid, while many hormonal responses develop more gradually and last longer.

Hypothalamus & Pituitary Gland

The hypothalamus and pituitary form a major control center for the endocrine system.

  • Hypothalamus: Detects internal conditions and releases signals that regulate pituitary activity.
  • Anterior Pituitary: Produces and releases several hormones, including growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and adrenocorticotropic hormone.
  • Posterior Pituitary: Stores and releases ADH and oxytocin, which are produced in the hypothalamus.

The pituitary is often called the master gland, although its activity is regulated by the hypothalamus and feedback from other glands.

Adrenal Glands

Each adrenal gland has two main regions:

  • Adrenal Cortex (Outer):
    • Cortisol: Helps regulate metabolism and the body’s response to stress.
    • Aldosterone: Promotes sodium retention and potassium excretion, helping regulate blood volume and pressure.
  • Adrenal Medulla (Inner):
    • Epinephrine and Norepinephrine: Support the fight-or-flight response.

Pancreas

The pancreas has both endocrine and digestive (exocrine) functions.

  • Beta Cells: Produce insulin, which lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake and storage.
  • Alpha Cells: Produce glucagon, which raises blood glucose by stimulating glucose release from the liver.

Key Processes

Hormonal Signaling

  • Hormone Secretion: Glands release hormones in response to internal signals.
  • Hormone Transport: Many hormones travel through the bloodstream to target tissues.
  • Hormone Receptors: Only cells with appropriate receptors respond to a particular hormone.
  • Feedback Regulation: Hormone production is adjusted according to the body’s needs.

Negative Feedback

Negative feedback counteracts a change to help maintain homeostasis.

Example — Blood Glucose Regulation:

  1. Blood glucose rises after eating.
  2. The pancreas releases insulin.
  3. Insulin promotes glucose uptake and storage.
  4. Blood glucose decreases toward its normal range.
  5. Insulin secretion decreases as the stimulus resolves.

When blood glucose falls, the pancreas increases glucagon secretion, helping the liver release glucose into the bloodstream.

Key Principle: Negative feedback is the most common endocrine regulatory mechanism.

Positive Feedback

Positive feedback amplifies a response until a particular event or endpoint occurs.

Example — Childbirth:

  1. The baby’s head stretches the cervix.
  2. Nerve signals stimulate oxytocin release.
  3. Oxytocin increases uterine contractions.
  4. Contractions increase cervical stretching.
  5. The cycle continues until delivery.

Oxytocin also causes milk ejection (let-down) during breastfeeding in response to suckling. Milk production itself is primarily stimulated by prolactin.

Other Important Functions

  • Metabolic Regulation: Thyroid hormones influence energy use and heat production.
  • Stress Response: Cortisol and catecholamines help the body respond to physical and emotional stress.
  • Fluid Balance: ADH reduces water loss through the kidneys; aldosterone supports sodium and fluid retention.
  • Calcium Regulation: PTH and vitamin D help maintain calcium balance.
  • Growth and Development: Growth hormone, thyroid hormones, and sex hormones influence body development.
  • Circadian Regulation: Melatonin helps coordinate sleep-wake timing.

Effects of Aging

  • Changes in Hormonal Regulation: Hormone levels and tissue sensitivity may change with age; not all hormones decrease.
  • Reduced Growth Hormone Secretion: May contribute to changes in body composition, alongside activity, nutrition, and other factors.
  • Reduced Insulin Sensitivity: Can increase the risk of type 2 diabetes.
  • Changes in Thyroid Regulation: Thyroid function and hormone metabolism may change, although significant thyroid disease is not inevitable.
  • Changes in Sex Hormones: Estrogen and progesterone decrease substantially after menopause; testosterone may decline gradually in some men.
  • Changes in Melatonin Secretion: May contribute to altered sleep-wake patterns.
  • Changes in Fluid Regulation: Altered thirst and ADH responses can increase vulnerability to fluid imbalance.

Clinical Implications

  • Greater vulnerability to abnormal blood glucose levels.
  • Changes in energy, weight, temperature tolerance, and sleep.
  • Increased importance of monitoring food and fluid intake when indicated.
  • Increased vulnerability to dehydration in some older adults.
  • Hormonal disorders may present with fatigue, weakness, confusion, or changes in daily functioning.

Important: Diabetes, thyroid disorders, severe hormonal deficiencies, and unexplained changes in weight or mental status are not inevitable consequences of aging.

Important Distinctions:

  • Type 1 vs. Type 2 Diabetes: Type 1 involves autoimmune insulin deficiency; type 2 involves insulin resistance and progressive impairment of insulin secretion.
  • Hypoglycemia vs. Hyperglycemia: Hypoglycemia means low blood glucose; hyperglycemia means high blood glucose.
  • Hypothyroidism vs. Hyperthyroidism: Hypothyroidism generally slows metabolic processes; hyperthyroidism generally accelerates them.
  • Cushing Syndrome vs. Adrenal Insufficiency: Excess cortisol versus insufficient cortisol.

CNAs observe and report endocrine abnormalities rather than independently diagnosing conditions or adjusting medications. Blood glucose testing or treatment assistance must follow the CNA’s authorized scope, training, and facility policy.

When to Report

  • New or worsening excessive thirst or frequent urination.
  • Sweating, shakiness, weakness, or sudden confusion.
  • Unexpected changes in appetite or body weight.
  • Unusual fatigue or declining activity tolerance.
  • Heat or cold intolerance.
  • New tremors or rapid heartbeat.
  • Dizziness, fainting, or changes in consciousness.
  • Blood glucose measurements outside prescribed parameters, when monitoring is an authorized assigned task.

Emergency signs: Severe confusion, seizure, unresponsiveness, or signs of a serious diabetic or adrenal emergency require immediate emergency response.

If hypoglycemia is suspected, follow the facility’s emergency protocol. Do not give food or liquids by mouth to someone who is unconscious or unable to swallow safely.

Clinical Connections

  • Vital Signs: Hormonal disorders may affect pulse, blood pressure, and temperature regulation.
  • Fluid Balance: ADH and aldosterone help regulate fluid balance.
  • Dehydration: Excessive urination or impaired fluid regulation can increase dehydration risk.
  • Assisting with Feeding: Meal timing and adequate intake are particularly important for residents with diabetes.
  • Recognizing Patient Distress: Severe glucose abnormalities can cause acute changes in consciousness.
  • Skin Integrity & Breakdown Prevention: Diabetes can increase vulnerability to slow-healing wounds.
  • Fall Risk: Weakness, dizziness, or hypoglycemia may increase fall risk.
