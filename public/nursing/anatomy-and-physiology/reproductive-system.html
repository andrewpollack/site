Reproductive System

Reproductive System

Oct 26, 202611 min read

Overview

The reproductive system produces reproductive cells (sperm and eggs), enables fertilization and reproduction, and produces sex hormones.

It works closely with the Endocrine System to regulate puberty, sexual development, fertility, pregnancy, and reproductive aging.

The reproductive system has three major functions:

  • Reproduction: Produces sperm and eggs and enables fertilization.
  • Hormone Production: Produces hormones such as testosterone, estrogen, and progesterone.
  • Sexual Development: Supports puberty, reproductive maturation, and secondary sex characteristics.

The female reproductive system also supports pregnancy, childbirth, and, through associated mammary glands, breastfeeding.

Major Structures

Male Reproductive System

Primary Reproductive Organs

  • Testes (Testicles): Two organs located in the scrotum.
    • Produce sperm through spermatogenesis.
    • Produce testosterone, the primary androgen involved in male sexual development.
  • Scrotum: External pouch that protects the testes and helps regulate their temperature.

Accessory Reproductive Structures

  • Epididymis: Coiled tube along each testis where sperm mature and are stored.
  • Vas Deferens: Muscular tube transporting sperm from the epididymis toward the ejaculatory ducts.
  • Seminal Vesicles: Glands producing fructose-rich fluid that nourishes sperm and contributes to semen.
  • Prostate Gland: Located below the bladder and surrounding part of the urethra; produces fluid that contributes to semen.
  • Bulbourethral (Cowper’s) Glands: Produce lubricating mucus released into the urethra.
  • Urethra: Tube carrying urine and, at different times, semen to the outside of the body.
  • Penis: External organ containing erectile tissue; enables sexual intercourse and ejaculation.

Sperm Pathway: Testes → Epididymis → Vas Deferens → Ejaculatory Ducts → Urethra → Outside the body.

Male Secondary Sex Characteristics

During puberty, increasing androgen activity contributes to:

  • Deepening of the voice.
  • Growth of facial, pubic, axillary, and sometimes chest hair.
  • Increased muscle mass and strength.
  • Broader shoulders and changes in body composition.

Female Reproductive System

Primary Reproductive Organs

  • Ovaries: Two organs located on either side of the uterus.
    • Contain developing egg cells (oocytes).
    • Release an oocyte during ovulation.
    • Produce estrogen and progesterone.

Internal Reproductive Structures

  • Fallopian (Uterine) Tubes: Transport the oocyte toward the uterus; fertilization most commonly occurs in the ampulla of a uterine tube.
  • Uterus: Muscular organ where an embryo may implant and develop during pregnancy.
    • Endometrium: Inner lining that changes during the menstrual cycle and is shed during menstruation.
    • Myometrium: Thick muscular layer that contracts during menstruation and childbirth.
    • Cervix: Lower portion of the uterus that opens into the vagina.
  • Vagina: Muscular canal connecting the cervix to the outside of the body; allows menstrual flow, intercourse, and childbirth.

External Reproductive Structures

The external female genital structures are collectively called the vulva.

  • Labia Majora: Outer skin folds that help protect the external genital structures.
  • Labia Minora: Inner folds surrounding the vestibule and openings.
  • Clitoris: Highly sensitive erectile organ involved in sexual arousal.
  • Vaginal Opening: External opening of the vaginal canal.
  • Urethral Opening: Separate opening through which urine exits the body.
  • Perineum: Region between the external genitalia and anus.

Associated Structures

  • Mammary Glands (Breasts): Produce milk after childbirth under hormonal regulation.

Female Secondary Sex Characteristics

During puberty, increasing estrogen activity contributes to:

  • Breast development.
  • Widening and development of the pelvis.
  • Increased fat distribution around the hips, thighs, and breasts.
  • Changes in body composition.
  • Pubic and axillary hair growth, primarily influenced by androgens.

Key Processes

Hormonal Regulation

The reproductive system is regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis.

  1. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH).
  2. The anterior pituitary gland releases follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH).
  3. The testes or ovaries respond by producing sex hormones and supporting reproductive cell development.
  4. Hormone feedback helps regulate this process.

Major Reproductive Hormones

  • Testosterone: Supports sperm production, sexual development, and male reproductive function.
  • Estrogen: Supports female reproductive development and regulates the menstrual cycle.
  • Progesterone: Helps prepare and maintain the uterine lining for pregnancy.
  • FSH: Supports ovarian follicle development and sperm production.
  • LH: Triggers ovulation and stimulates testosterone production in the testes.

Sperm Production & Ejaculation

  • Spermatogenesis: Production of sperm in the testes.
  • Sperm Maturation: Sperm mature in the epididymis.
  • Ejaculation: Muscular contractions propel semen through the urethra.
  • Semen: Mixture of sperm and fluids from reproductive glands.

Menstrual Cycle

The menstrual cycle prepares the female reproductive system for a possible pregnancy.

  • Menstruation: Shedding of the uterine lining when pregnancy has not occurred.
  • Follicular Phase: Ovarian follicles develop, and estrogen levels generally rise.
  • Ovulation: An oocyte is released from an ovary, triggered by an LH surge.
  • Luteal Phase: The corpus luteum produces progesterone to support the uterine lining.

If pregnancy does not occur, estrogen and progesterone levels fall, and menstruation begins.

Fertilization & Pregnancy

  • Fertilization: Sperm and oocyte combine, usually within a fallopian tube, forming a zygote.
  • Implantation: The developing embryo attaches to the uterine lining.
  • Pregnancy: The uterus supports fetal development, while the placenta facilitates nutrient, gas, and waste exchange between maternal and fetal circulations.
  • Childbirth: Uterine contractions help deliver the baby through the cervix and vagina.

Breastfeeding

  • Prolactin: Stimulates milk production.
  • Oxytocin: Causes milk ejection (let-down) and contributes to uterine contractions.

Effects of Aging

Male Reproductive Changes

  • Changes in Testosterone: Testosterone levels may gradually decline with age, although changes vary considerably.
  • Changes in Sexual Function: Erectile function and ejaculation may change due to vascular, neurological, hormonal, or medication-related factors.
  • Changes in Sperm Production: Sperm production can continue later in life, although fertility may decline.
  • Prostate Enlargement: Benign prostatic hyperplasia becomes more common with age and may affect urination.

Female Reproductive Changes

  • Perimenopause: Transitional period during which menstrual cycles and hormone levels become increasingly variable.
  • Menopause: Permanent cessation of menstrual periods, confirmed after 12 consecutive months without menstruation when no other cause is present.
  • Reduced Estrogen: Can contribute to vaginal dryness, thinning of vulvovaginal tissues, and changes in urinary symptoms.
  • Changes in Bone Health: Reduced estrogen after menopause contributes to increased bone loss.
  • Changes in Pelvic Support: Pelvic floor weakness may contribute to pelvic organ prolapse or urinary incontinence.

Clinical Implications

  • Changes in hormone levels can affect sexual function, comfort, and reproductive health.
  • Vaginal dryness or fragile tissues may cause discomfort during hygiene and personal care.
  • Prostate enlargement can contribute to urinary hesitancy, weak stream, and retention.
  • Pelvic floor weakness may affect bladder control.
  • Older adults may remain sexually active and require appropriate privacy, dignity, and health education.

Important: Sexual activity and interest do not necessarily disappear with aging. Pain, unexplained bleeding, severe urinary symptoms, or sudden changes in reproductive health should not be dismissed as normal aging.

Important Distinctions

  • BPH vs. Prostate Cancer: BPH is noncancerous prostate enlargement; prostate cancer involves malignant cell growth. Both may affect urinary function.
  • Cystocele vs. Uterine Prolapse: A cystocele involves the bladder bulging into the vaginal wall; uterine prolapse involves descent of the uterus.
  • Vaginitis vs. Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause: Vaginitis describes vaginal inflammation from several possible causes; GSM describes changes primarily related to low estrogen.
  • Menopause vs. Reproductive Disease: Menopause is a normal biological transition, not an illness.
  • STI vs. Vaginitis: STIs are transmitted through sexual contact; vaginitis can have infectious or noninfectious causes and is not always sexually transmitted.

Important: CNAs observe and report reproductive abnormalities rather than independently diagnosing conditions or choosing treatments.

When to Report

  • New genital pain, irritation, swelling, or unusual discharge.
  • Abnormal vaginal bleeding, especially bleeding after menopause.
  • New difficulty urinating or suspected urinary retention.
  • New pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort.
  • Genital sores, lesions, or unexplained skin changes.
  • New lumps or swelling in the breast, testicles, or genital area.
  • Fever associated with pelvic or genital symptoms.
  • Any concerning change from the resident’s usual baseline.

Emergency signs: Sudden severe testicular or pelvic pain, heavy bleeding with signs of shock, or acute inability to urinate with severe pain requires urgent or emergency evaluation.

Clinical Connections

  • Perineal Care: Proper hygiene supports comfort, skin integrity, and dignity.
  • Incontinence Care: Urinary changes may be associated with prostate enlargement or pelvic floor dysfunction.
  • Elimination Care: Reproductive and urinary anatomy are closely related.
  • Skin Integrity & Breakdown Prevention: Genital irritation and moisture exposure may contribute to skin problems.
  • Therapeutic Communication: Reproductive and sexual health concerns require respectful, private communication.
  • Patient Rights: Residents retain rights to privacy, dignity, bodily autonomy, and consensual intimacy, subject to applicable safeguards.
  • Abuse, Neglect & Rights Violations: Recognize and report suspected sexual abuse according to mandatory reporting and facility procedures.
