Nervous System

Nervous System

Oct 26, 20269 min read

Overview

The nervous system receives sensory information, processes it, and coordinates the body’s responses.

It controls movement, sensation, thinking, memory, emotions, and involuntary functions such as breathing, heart rate, and digestion.

The two main anatomical divisions are:

  • Central Nervous System (CNS): Brain and spinal cord.
  • Peripheral Nervous System (PNS): Nerves and ganglia outside the CNS that carry information between the CNS and the body.

The nervous system works closely with the Sensory Organs to detect and interpret changes in the environment.

Major Structures

Central Nervous System (CNS)

  • Brain: Main control center responsible for processing information and coordinating body functions.
    • Cerebrum: Conscious thought, memory, language, sensory interpretation, and voluntary movement.
    • Cerebellum: Coordination, balance, posture, and motor learning.
    • Brainstem: Connects the brain with the spinal cord and helps regulate essential functions.
      • Midbrain: Movement control and visual and auditory reflexes.
      • Pons: Breathing regulation, sleep, and communication between brain regions.
      • Medulla Oblongata: Breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, and protective reflexes.
    • Diencephalon: Region containing structures including the thalamus and hypothalamus.
      • Thalamus: Relays most sensory information to the cerebral cortex.
      • Hypothalamus: Regulates temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep, and endocrine activity.
  • Spinal Cord: Transmits signals between the brain and body and coordinates many reflexes.
  • Meninges: Three protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord.
  • Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF): Cushions the CNS and helps maintain its environment.

Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)

  • Cranial Nerves: Twelve pairs of nerves associated with the brain and brainstem that support functions such as facial sensation, vision, eye movement, hearing, and swallowing.
  • Spinal Nerves: Thirty-one pairs of nerves connecting the spinal cord to the body.
  • Sensory (Afferent) Nerves: Carry information from receptors toward the CNS.
  • Motor (Efferent) Nerves: Carry commands from the CNS toward muscles and glands.

Functional Divisions

  • Somatic Nervous System: Supports conscious sensation and voluntary skeletal muscle movement.
  • Autonomic Nervous System (ANS): Regulates involuntary functions such as heart rate, digestion, and blood vessel diameter.
    • Sympathetic: Supports the fight-or-flight response.
    • Parasympathetic: Supports rest-and-digest functions.
    • Enteric Nervous System: Network of neurons within the gastrointestinal tract that helps regulate digestion.

Nervous Tissue

  • Neurons: Cells specialized for transmitting electrical and chemical signals.
    • Dendrites: Receive incoming information.
    • Cell Body: Contains the nucleus and supports the neuron.
    • Axon: Conducts nerve impulses away from the cell body.
    • Myelin Sheath: Insulates many axons and increases signal conduction speed.
  • Synapses: Junctions where neurons communicate with other cells.
  • Neurotransmitters: Chemical messengers used for communication at many synapses.
  • Glial Cells: Cells that support, protect, nourish, and insulate neurons.

Key Processes

  • Sensory Reception: Specialized receptors detect stimuli such as light, sound, touch, temperature, and pain.
  • Sensory Processing: The CNS interprets information received from sensory receptors.
  • Motor Control: Nerve signals activate muscles to produce movement.
  • Reflexes: Rapid, often automatic responses to stimuli; some involve the spinal cord without requiring initial conscious processing.
  • Nerve Impulse Transmission: Electrical signals called action potentials travel along neurons.
  • Synaptic Transmission: Signals pass between cells through neurotransmitters or electrical connections.
  • Autonomic Regulation: Controls many involuntary activities.
  • Homeostasis: Helps maintain stable internal conditions.
  • Learning and Memory: Neural networks acquire, retain, and retrieve information.
  • Neuroplasticity: Neural connections can change through experience, learning, and recovery.

Effects of Aging

  • Slower Reaction Times: Responses to stimuli may take longer.
  • Changes in Sensation: Some people experience reduced sensitivity to touch, vibration, or temperature.
  • Changes in Balance: Altered sensory processing and motor control may affect stability.
  • Sleep Changes: Sleep patterns and circadian rhythms may change.
  • Changes in Memory Processing: Some forms of recall and information processing may become slower.
  • Reduced Autonomic Responsiveness: Blood pressure regulation during position changes may become less effective.

Clinical Implications

  • Increased risk of falls when balance, coordination, or reaction time is impaired.
  • More time may be needed for communication and movement.
  • Sensory impairments may affect safety and independence.
  • Changes in position may increase dizziness in some residents.
  • Acute illness can increase vulnerability to delirium.

Important: Dementia, sudden confusion, new paralysis, and major neurological deficits are not normal consequences of aging.

Important Distinctions

  • Dementia vs. Alzheimer’s disease: Dementia is a syndrome; Alzheimer’s is one possible cause.
  • Delirium vs. dementia: Delirium develops acutely and often fluctuates; dementia usually progresses more gradually.
  • Stroke vs. TIA: Stroke causes brain infarction or hemorrhage; TIA causes transient ischemic symptoms without acute infarction. Both require emergency evaluation.
  • SCI vs. TBI: Spinal cord injury primarily affects spinal nerve pathways; traumatic brain injury affects the brain. Trauma can cause both.
  • Seizures: Not all seizures involve convulsions; some involve staring, altered awareness, or unusual movements.

Important: CNAs observe, support, and report rather than independently diagnosing neurological disorders.

When to Report

  • New confusion, disorientation, or unexpected behavior.
  • New weakness, numbness, tingling, or loss of sensation.
  • Changes in walking, balance, or coordination.
  • New tremors, unusual movements, or seizure activity.
  • Changes in speech, vision, or swallowing.
  • Severe or unusual headaches.
  • Changes in level of consciousness.
  • Sudden decline in normal function.

Emergency signs: Sudden facial drooping, one-sided weakness, speech difficulty, sudden severe neurological symptoms, unresponsiveness, or a prolonged seizure require immediate emergency response.

Clinical Connections

  • Dementia Care: Cognitive changes may affect memory, communication, and independence.
  • Neurological Observations: Changes in alertness, speech, movement, or sensation may indicate neurological illness.
  • Fall Risk: Impaired balance, coordination, or sensation can increase fall risk.
  • Recognizing Patient Distress: Sudden neurological symptoms may indicate an emergency.
  • Vital Signs: Neurological illness may affect or accompany changes in vital signs.
  • Therapeutic Communication: Adapt communication for cognitive, language, or sensory difficulties.
  • Safe Patient Transfers: Weakness and impaired coordination affect mobility safety.
